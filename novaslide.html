Case study · 2025

NOVA

AR navigation for children that balances independence and parental reassurance.

The bet

A child will wear safety tech only if it feels like freedom

A CCA capstone with the UNICEF Innovation Node, designing how children will move through cities in the next 3 to 10 years. The safest system on paper is worthless if the child takes it off, so every design call had to buy the parent reassurance without costing the child independence.

Problem

Unsafe conditions for children moving through the city alone

01

Global burden

Road traffic injuries are the leading cause of death for children ages 5 to 12. Nearly 220,000 lives lost every year, mostly where road safety infrastructure is limited.

02

Preventable injuries

More than 1,600 children and adolescents die daily from preventable injuries, most tied to unsafe walking conditions and missing protective infrastructure near schools.

03

Parental concerns

1 in 5 US parents have never allowed their teen to travel alone, citing traffic, unfamiliar routes, and abduction fears.

Solution

AR guidance for children, safety controls for parents

Nova pairs AR glasses a child wears with a companion app the parent holds. The system supports safe, independent mobility while keeping families connected.

01

For children

The glasses provide simple HUD text, responsive navigation cues, and a friendly mascot that guides them safely while encouraging awareness of the street.

02

For parents

The companion app sets up child profiles, safe zones, and emergency contacts. Alerts fire only when a boundary is crossed, reassurance without constant surveillance.

Research

Grounding the design in real experiences

Expert events, family fieldwork at UC Berkeley and the SF Summer Resource Fair, five parent interviews, and co-creation workshops with families.

Research · Insights

Three findings set the direction

01

The independence tension

Parents want reassurance and kids want independence. Every mobility decision sits in that tension.

02

System change is slow

Policy and infrastructure change over years, so families need something practical they can use now.

03

AR is becoming everyday

Lightweight wearables are entering everyday life, opening a window for AR that balances safety with freedom.

We scored five mobility solutions against the same criteria, GPS trackers to community escorts. AR glasses scored strongest, a forward-looking direction as AR, language models, and hardware mature.

Design statement

Four things at once

The glasses had to do four jobs simultaneously, and dropping any one of them breaks the product.

01

Guide safely

Turn-by-turn cues and hazard alerts a child can follow through public space.

02

Build independence

Confidence and awareness, not dependence on the device.

03

Reassure parents

Enough visibility that a parent lets the trip happen at all.

04

Stay simple

Everyday usability for a child, nothing to fiddle with.

HUD text

The winning style in action. Alert on the left, time and distance on the right, mascot and path in the scene, all in fixed positions a child can read without losing the street.

Independence vs tracking

The feature parents wanted would cost us the wearer

Some parents asked for full real-time tracking. The children said they would not wear the glasses if every step was visible. So tracking became a setting, safe zones stayed the default, and alerts fire only when a boundary is crossed.

The mascot

Nova makes it engaging without stealing attention

Minimal AR visuals reduced distraction but risked feeling plain. Nova, a friendly guide with strong contrast and a reassuring presence, adds warmth without crowding the view. Left: without. Right: with Nova.

The mascot · Exploration

Exploring who Nova should be

Nova means new star, guidance and growth, in line with UNICEF's mission. The character was drawn until it read with strong contrast and playful clarity, a reassuring presence that never overwhelms the street.

Usability testing

20 parents, one month, sometimes with their children

A one month prototype sprint sharpened the navigation cues, the safety alerts, and the companion app setup, and pushed the hardware concept toward thinner, more child-friendly glasses.

Final Design · 1 of 3

Companion app onboarding

Parents connect the glasses, set up a child profile, add emergency contacts, and define safe zones like home or school. Alerts arrive only if the child leaves a safe area.

Final Design · 2 of 3

Navigation mode

Clear voice prompts and simple visual cues guide the trip to school turn by turn, with contextual alerts for construction and traffic lights, and small celebrations on arrival.

Final Design · 3 of 3

Freedom mode

As children explore on their own, the glasses warn when they leave a safe area, discourage unsafe interactions, and guide them back to safety. Independence, with a quiet net under it.

Reflection

The most protective version was the one no child would wear

The safest system on paper gave parents everything they asked for, and it was the one the children told us they would take off. A product a child refuses to wear delivers zero safety. That is why tracking is a setting and safe zones are the default.

When two users want opposite things, the default is the design decision, not the feature list.
